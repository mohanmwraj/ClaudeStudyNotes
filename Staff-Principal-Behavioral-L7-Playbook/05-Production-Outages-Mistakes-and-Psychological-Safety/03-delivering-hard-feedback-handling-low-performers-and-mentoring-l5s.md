# 05.3 · Delivering Hard Feedback, Handling Low Performers and Mentoring L5s

> **Folder 05 · File 3 of 3.** Staff and Principal engineers aren't managers, but they're asked people questions in every senior loop: "Tell me about difficult feedback you gave", "How did you handle someone on your project who wasn't performing?", "Who have you grown?" These questions test whether your leverage extends to **people**, the third of Reilly's pillars ("leveling up"). They also test whether you understand **the boundary of your role**: an IC who manages performance behind the manager's back is as much of a concern as one who avoids it.
>
> This file covers:
> - feedback models that work (SBI, Radical Candor) and feedback across levels: to peers, seniors and upward;
> - low performance from an IC's seat: diagnosis, partnership with the manager, and the hero-absorbs trap;
> - mentoring, coaching and sponsorship, and how an L7 grows L5s into Staff;
> - measuring people leverage;
> - a rubric, three story blueprints and five follow-up chains.
>
> Stories are **illustrative composites**.

---

## 1. Feedback that works

### 1.1 Two models worth naming

| Model | Core idea | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| **SBI** (Center for Creative Leadership): Situation, Behavior, Impact | Describe a specific situation, the observable behavior, and its impact. CCL extends it to SBII, adding an inquiry into the person's intent. | Making feedback specific and non-judgmental |
| **Radical Candor** (Kim Scott, 2017) | Care personally *and* challenge directly. Without caring, it's obnoxious aggression; without challenging, it's ruinous empathy. | The stance: why you give feedback at all |

An SBI example: "In yesterday's design review (situation), you rewrote Priya's proposal on the whiteboard before she'd finished presenting (behavior). She didn't speak again, and we didn't hear her reasons for the queue design, which I think were good (impact). What were you aiming for at that point? (intent)"

### 1.2 Feedback across levels

| Direction | What's different | L7 practice |
|---|---|---|
| **To juniors** | Power asymmetry: your words land heavily | Specific, private, frequent; separate growth feedback from evaluation |
| **To peers** | No authority; reputational stakes | Direct and early; offer it as your observation, with evidence; ask for theirs |
| **To more senior engineers** | They may not expect it | Ask permission, be specific, assume they have context you don't |
| **Upward to managers and directors** | Risk to you | Frame around shared goals; bring data; offer it privately |
| **About someone to their manager** | Can feel like going around them | Give it to the person first; tell them you'll share it with their manager if it continues; never as a surprise |

The common thread: **say it to the person first.** Feedback that reaches someone through their manager, or through a review, when you could have said it directly is a candor failure (02.3, Netflix).

## 2. Low performance, from an IC's seat

### 2.1 The role boundary

The manager owns performance management: expectations in the formal sense, reviews, improvement plans and exits. The senior IC owns **the outcome of the work they lead**, and often has better information about the day-to-day work than the manager does. The L7 behavior is a **partnership**: you observe, diagnose and support; you share what you see with the manager, transparently; you don't run a shadow performance process.

### 2.2 Diagnose before acting

| Cause | Signs | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| **Skill** | Wants to, can't yet | Pairing, smaller scoped tasks, explicit teaching |
| **Context** | Unclear expectations, missing domain knowledge, moved to an unfamiliar area | Clear written expectations; onboarding; a domain guide |
| **Fit** | Strong elsewhere, struggling in this kind of work | A conversation with the manager about a different role |
| **Motivation** | Disengaged | Understanding why: career, recognition, the work itself |
| **Circumstances** | Personal or health issues | Space and support; involve the manager; never pry |

Most "low performers" on senior-led projects are **context** problems. That's why diagnosis comes first: treating a context problem as a skill problem insults the person, and treating it as a motivation problem damages them.

### 2.3 The hero-absorbs trap

The most common senior-IC failure in this area: you quietly do the struggling person's work yourself. It feels kind and it protects the deadline. It also:
- hides the problem from the manager;
- denies the person the feedback they need;
- makes you the bottleneck;
- and tells the committee you avoid hard conversations.

"I picked up his tasks so we'd hit the date" is a **down-level sentence** at L6/L7.

### 2.4 When it doesn't work out

Not every story ends with improvement. An honest story where the person moved to a role that suited them better, or left, can be **strong**, if you handled it with candor and care, and kept the manager in the loop throughout. Interviewers trust a candidate more if they can tell one story where things didn't get better.

## 3. Growing L5s into Staff

### 3.1 Mentoring, coaching and sponsorship

| Mode | What you do | What it builds |
|---|---|---|
| **Mentoring** | Share your experience and advice | Knowledge |
| **Coaching** | Ask questions so they find the answer | Judgment |
| **Sponsorship** | Spend your own credibility to get them opportunities and visibility: name them for the project, put them in the room, advocate in calibration | Scope, and the evidence a promotion needs |

Most senior engineers mentor. Far fewer sponsor, and **sponsorship is what grows Staff engineers**, because a promotion to Staff needs Staff-scope evidence, and an L5 rarely gets Staff-scope work without someone senior making room for it.

### 3.2 How an L7 grows an L5 into a Staff engineer

1. **Give them a problem, not a task.** Ownership of an outcome with ambiguity in it: "make cross-team schema changes safe", not "build the schema checker".
2. **Delegate the decision, not just the work.** They write the design and make the calls; you review, you don't decide.
3. **Stretch with a safety net.** Agree in advance which decisions they'll bring to you (the one-way doors) and which they won't.
4. **Put them in the rooms.** They present at the architecture review and to the director, not you.
5. **Step back visibly.** When people come to you about their area, send them to the owner.
6. **Advocate with evidence.** In calibration or promotion discussions, give specific examples of Staff-scope impact.

### 3.3 Measuring people leverage

| Weak evidence | Strong evidence |
|---|---|
| "I mentored several engineers" | Named people, what they now own, and what they did without you |
| "They grew a lot" | Promotions, with what the case was built on |
| "I run office hours" | A practice adopted beyond your team, run by others |
| "People come to me for advice" | People who used to come to you now go to someone you grew |

## 4. Hiring Committee Scoring Rubric

| Signal | Strong Hire at L7 | Hire at L6 | Down-level to L5 | No hire |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Feedback** | Direct, specific feedback to a peer or more senior engineer that changed behavior; received hard feedback and changed | Clear feedback to engineers across teams | Feedback to juniors only | Avoids, or routes through others |
| **Low performance** | Diagnosed the cause; partnered transparently with the manager; adjusted context; honest outcome even if not positive | Supported the person and involved the manager | Absorbed the work | Went around the manager, or complained |
| **Role boundary** | Clear on what the manager owns and what they own; never a shadow process | Mostly clear | Blurry | Acted as manager without the role |
| **Sponsorship** | Grew L5s into owners of Staff-scope problems; promotions with specific evidence | Mentored engineers across teams | Mentored on own team | None |
| **Leverage** | People they grew are now growing others | Some named outcomes | Generic | None |
| **Empathy and candor** | Both, visibly in the same story | Balanced | One without the other | Neither |

## 5. Story Blueprints

### 5.1 Hard feedback to a senior peer (composite: harsh code reviews)

**The facts (composite):** a respected senior engineer's code reviews are technically excellent and harsh. Two junior engineers have asked to move teams; review turnaround in the area is slow because people avoid requesting his review.

#### How an L5 tells it

> "There was a senior engineer whose code reviews were really harsh, and some juniors were upset. I mentioned it to our manager, who talked to him. His reviews got a bit better after that."

**What the committee extracts:** feedback routed through the manager. **L5, and a candor concern.**

#### How an L7 tells it

> "Daniel was the most knowledgeable engineer on our storage layer, and his code reviews were technically the best in the org. They were also harsh: two junior engineers had asked to move teams, and when I looked at the review data, people were routing around him, so his area had the slowest review turnaround we had.
>
> I talked to him directly, privately, with specifics. I described one review: on a junior's first pull request, he'd left 40 comments, with no distinction between blocking issues and preferences, and one read 'Did you even run this?'. I told him the impact: she hadn't opened a pull request in his area since, and turnaround in his area had doubled. Then I asked what he'd been aiming for.
>
> His answer changed my picture. He'd been burned by two production incidents that came from changes nobody reviewed carefully, and he believed being exhaustive was the only safe option. That was a real concern, so we worked on it together, not just on his tone. We agreed a review convention for the team: comments labeled 'blocking' or 'suggestion', a limit of one round of style comments on a first PR, and a checklist that captured the things he always looked for, so other reviewers could catch them too.
>
> He adopted it, and he went further than I'd asked: he started pairing with new engineers on their first change in his area. Within a quarter, review turnaround in his area went from the slowest in the org to about median. The junior engineer who'd stopped submitting there became the second reviewer for that code. The labeling convention spread to the rest of the org. Daniel and I talk about reviews regularly; he's the person who told me last year that my own design docs were too long."

**What the committee extracts:** direct, private, specific (SBI with intent); used data; discovered a legitimate underlying concern; solved the concern, not just the behavior; structural fix; measurable outcome; feedback is now two-way. **L7 candor with empathy.**

### 5.2 Low performance on a project you lead (composite: a mismatch of context)

**The facts (composite):** an engineer on the candidate's cross-team project is missing commitments repeatedly. He moved from a frontend team to distributed-systems work three months earlier.

#### How an L5 tells it

> "One engineer on my project wasn't keeping up and kept missing deadlines. To make sure we hit our date, I took over some of his tasks and did them myself. We shipped on time, and I let his manager know at the end that he'd struggled."

**What the committee extracts:** the hero-absorbs trap; the manager learned at the end; no diagnosis; no feedback. **Down-level.**

#### How an L7 tells it

> "Three weeks into a cross-team project I was leading, Arun had missed two commitments, and his pull requests were coming back with correctness problems in the consensus code. The easy option was to take his tasks myself. I didn't, because that would have hidden the problem and denied him the feedback.
>
> I started by understanding it. In a one-to-one, I asked how the work was going, and learned he'd moved from frontend to distributed systems three months earlier with no real onboarding. He'd been assigned the hardest part of the project, and he was embarrassed to say he didn't know what a fencing token was. That was a context problem, not a skill or motivation problem.
>
> I told him plainly that the two missed commitments were a problem for the project, and that I thought the cause was the assignment, not him. I also told him I'd be talking to his manager, and what I'd say. With his manager, I shared what I'd seen and what I proposed: move Arun off consensus onto the client-side retry layer, which was still substantial and closer to what he knew, and pair him with an engineer from the storage team for two weeks. His manager agreed, and kept owning the performance conversation itself.
>
> The client layer shipped on time and was one of the cleanest parts of the project. A year later, Arun took on a distributed-systems component on his own. Not every case like this ends well, and I've had one where the right answer was a move to a different team. In this one the problem was the assignment. The change I made for my own projects: I now ask each engineer, when I assign work, what's new to them in it, before the first deadline rather than after the second miss."

**What the committee extracts:** refused the hero-absorbs trap; diagnosed the cause; candid with the person about impact; transparent with the manager, who kept ownership of performance; adjusted context; outcome; acknowledged not every case ends well; a process change. **L7.**

### 5.3 Growing an L5 into a Staff engineer (composite: schema safety as a Staff problem)

#### How an L5 tells it

> "I mentored a mid-level engineer on my team. We had weekly one-on-ones where I gave her advice on design and career. She got promoted to senior last year, and I like to think I contributed to that."

**What the committee extracts:** mentoring, with no specifics and no sponsorship. **L5.**

#### How an L7 tells it

> "Mei was a strong L5 on the data platform, and she was clearly ready for more scope, but her work was all inside one team, so there was nothing a Staff promotion case could stand on. I had a problem I'd planned to take on myself: schema changes in our shared event streams broke downstream consumers about twice a quarter, across eight teams. It had ambiguity, cross-team friction and no owner, which is exactly the shape of Staff work.
>
> I gave it to her as a problem, not a task: 'make schema changes safe across the org'. I told her which decisions I wanted to see before she made them, which were the compatibility policy and anything that changed other teams' release processes, and that everything else was hers. She wrote the design. My review comments were questions, not edits.
>
> The hard moment was the architecture review, where a senior engineer from the analytics org pushed back on blocking incompatible changes in CI. I didn't answer for her. Afterwards, I asked her what she thought he was really worried about. She worked out it was emergency fixes, and came back with an override path that requires a second approver. He supported it.
>
> She presented the result to the director herself, and when teams came to me about schema questions, I sent them to her. Within two quarters, schema-related consumer breakages went from about two a quarter to zero, and the compatibility check runs on all eight teams' streams. In her promotion discussion I gave three specific examples of Staff-scope impact, with the review pushback among them. She was promoted to Staff the next cycle, and she's now mentoring an L5 on the platform team through a problem with the same shape."

**What the committee extracts:** recognized the evidence gap; sponsored by giving away a Staff-shaped problem the candidate could have owned; delegated decisions with an explicit safety net; didn't rescue her in the hard moment; put her in the rooms; redirected traffic to her; specific advocacy; promotion; the leverage is recursive. **Strong L7 sponsorship.**

## 6. Adversarial Bar-Raiser Follow-Up Chains

### Chain A · "Did you say it to them directly?" 🔥

| # | Probe | Tests | Passenger | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Who did you tell first?" | Candor | "My manager" | "Him, privately" |
| 2 | "What exactly did you say?" | Specificity | Vague | The situation, behavior, impact and question |
| 3 | "How did he react?" | Honesty | "He accepted it" | His real reaction, including the concern behind it |
| 4 | "What did you learn from him?" | Two-way | Nothing | His underlying concern about incidents |
| 5 | "Has he given you feedback since?" | Relationship | No | A specific example |

### Chain B · Low performance and the manager

| # | Probe | Tests | Passenger | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "When did his manager find out?" | Transparency | "At the end" | Early, with the person told first |
| 2 | "Who owned the performance conversation?" | Role boundary | "Me" | "His manager. I owned the project and the assignment" |
| 3 | "Why didn't you just do the work yourself?" | Hero trap | "I did" | Why that would have hidden the problem |
| 4 | "What was the cause?" | Diagnosis | "He wasn't good enough" | Context, with the evidence |
| 5 | "Tell me about a time it didn't work out." | Realism | None | An honest case and how it was handled |

### Chain C · Sponsorship 🔥

| # | Probe | Tests | Passenger | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "What did you give up so she could grow?" | Sponsorship | Nothing | A Staff-shaped problem the candidate would have owned |
| 2 | "Which decisions did you keep?" | Safety net | All or none | The compatibility policy and cross-team process changes |
| 3 | "When did you want to step in, and didn't?" | Restraint | Never | The architecture review |
| 4 | "What did you say in her promotion discussion?" | Advocacy | "I supported her" | Three specific examples |
| 5 | "What is she doing now?" | Recursive leverage | Unknown | Mentoring an L5 through the same kind of problem |

### Chain D · Receiving feedback

| # | Probe | Tests | Passenger | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "What's the hardest feedback you've received?" | Self-awareness | Mild | Something that stung |
| 2 | "Was it fair?" | Openness | "Not really" | Honest: partly or fully |
| 3 | "What did you change?" | Action | Generic | A specific, observable change |
| 4 | "How would that person describe you now?" | Evidence | Unsure | Plausibly, with evidence |

### Chain E · Role boundaries

| # | Probe | Tests | Passenger | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Isn't that the manager's job?" | Boundaries | "Yes, so I left it" or "I did it anyway" | What's the manager's, what's yours, and how you partnered |
| 2 | "Have you ever disagreed with a manager about someone's performance?" | Courage | No | A specific case, handled through the manager |
| 3 | "How do you give input to calibration?" | Process | Don't | Specific, evidence-based input when asked |

## 7. Checklist

- [ ] A feedback story where you spoke to a peer or more senior engineer directly, with SBI-level specifics.
- [ ] The feedback story includes what you learned from them.
- [ ] A low-performance story with a diagnosis, early transparency with the manager, and no hero-absorbs.
- [ ] You can say what the manager owned and what you owned.
- [ ] One people story with an honest, not-entirely-positive outcome.
- [ ] A sponsorship story: what you gave up, which decisions you kept, where you held back, and what the person now owns.
- [ ] People leverage measured: named people, what they own, and whom they're growing.
