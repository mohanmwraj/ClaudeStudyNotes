# 08.2 · The Two-Minute Pitch, and Reverse-Interview Questions for VPs and Directors

> **Folder 08 · File 2 of 3.** Two parts of a senior loop are routinely under-prepared because they feel like formalities: **"Tell me about yourself"** at the start, and **"What questions do you have for me?"** at the end. Neither is a formality at L7.
> - The opening frames how the interviewer hears everything after it. A two-minute pitch that establishes your archetype and scope means each later story lands as confirmation, not discovery.
> - The questions you ask a VP or director are scored, sometimes explicitly. They show whether you think about an organization's strategy and problems the way the role requires, and they're your best chance to find out whether the role is real.
>
> This file covers:
> - the structure of an L7 pitch, with time budgets and variants;
> - "why are you leaving?", "why this level?" and the first-90-days question;
> - reverse-interview questions by category, what to listen for in the answers, and the red flags;
> - a rubric, three story blueprints and follow-up chains.
>
> Pitches and answers below are **illustrative composites**.

---

## 1. The two-minute pitch

### 1.1 Structure

| Beat | Time | Content | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Identity** | 15 s | One sentence: archetype (01.2), domain, and the kind of problem you're known for | "I'm a principal engineer who works on the reliability and cost of large commerce platforms, mostly as an architect across several teams." |
| **2. Arc** | 45 s | Two or three career beats, each showing scope growing, with the reason you moved | "I started on payments… moved to lead the platform group because… then took on the cross-org…" |
| **3. Proof** | 40 s | One flagship headline (story 1, 08.1) with its business number | "Most recently I found that our marketplace launch would breach our inventory-accuracy commitments, and led the platform that eight teams now run on…" |
| **4. Now** | 20 s | What you're looking for, and why this role fits it | "I'm looking for an org-level problem in data platforms, which is why this role caught my attention." |

### 1.2 Rules

- **Scope in every beat.** Each sentence should say *how big* (teams, orgs, money, customers), not only *what*.
- **One number in the proof beat**, the one you're most confident of. More numbers dilute it.
- **End on the role.** The last sentence should give the interviewer a reason to connect you to their problem.
- **Not a CV walk.** Skip jobs that don't advance the arc.
- **Rehearsed, not memorized.** Know the beats; let the words vary.

### 1.3 Variants

| Audience | Adjust |
|---|---|
| **Hiring manager** | More on what you want next and why this team |
| **Bar raiser or behavioral interviewer** | More on scope and ownership; the proof beat matters most |
| **VP or director** | More on business outcomes and the kind of problem you take off a leader's plate |
| **Peer engineer** | More on technical depth and how you work with teams |
| **30-second version** | Identity, one proof sentence and the "now" sentence |

## 2. The other framing questions

### 2.1 "Why are you leaving?"

Rules: true, positive, forward-looking, and short. Never criticize your current employer.
- Strong forms: "the problems I'm best at are now solved there", "I want an org-level problem in X, and there isn't one where I am", "the company's direction changed, and the work I want isn't on the roadmap".
- Avoid: compensation as the first answer, a bad manager, a reorganization described as unfair.

### 2.2 "Why this level?"

The interviewer is testing calibration (01.1 §9). The answer is evidence of operating at the level now, not aspiration:
> "Most of my last two years' work has been at org scope: technical direction for four teams' data platform, and decisions that changed what our VP funded. I'm interviewing at the level that describes the work I'm already doing."

If you're targeting a stretch, say so honestly: it builds trust and often leads to a better conversation about the level.

### 2.3 "What would you do in your first 90 days?"

A common senior question. The strong shape is **listen, then find the problem, then deliver one early result**, not "rewrite the architecture":

| Period | Focus | Output |
|---|---|---|
| **Days 1–30** | Listen: one-to-ones with every TL and key partner; read the incident history, the roadmap, the cost data and the last year's decisions | A written map of the org's top technical risks and opportunities, checked with your manager |
| **Days 31–60** | Choose: one problem that's important, unowned and tractable | A proposal with a first proof point |
| **Days 61–90** | Deliver the first proof point; start the longer program | An early result, and credibility for the next step |

Respect What Came Before (02.1 §1.2) applies: an answer that starts with what you'd change before you've listened is a red flag.

## 3. Reverse-interview questions for VPs and directors

### 3.1 By category

| Category | Question (original formulation) | What you learn |
|---|---|---|
| **Strategy** | "What does the business need from this org in the next two years that it can't do today?" | Whether there's an L7-sized problem |
| | "Which technical bet are you least sure about?" | How leadership thinks about risk; whether candor is normal |
| **The role** | "What would make you say, a year from now, that hiring for this role was one of your best decisions?" | The real success criteria |
| | "Why is this role open now?" | Whether it's new, a replacement, or a rescue |
| | "What does a Staff or Principal engineer here do that a strong senior doesn't?" | Whether the level is understood |
| **Decision-making** | "How does a contested technical decision get made here? Can you give me a recent example?" | Decision rights; whether senior ICs are in the room |
| | "Tell me about a time a senior engineer changed your mind." | Whether IC influence is real |
| **Org health** | "What's the biggest source of friction between your teams?" | Where the alignment work will be |
| | "How have Staff+ engineers here been promoted recently, and for what?" | Whether there's a path, and what it values |
| **Failure** | "What's a recent incident or project failure, and what changed afterwards?" | Learning culture; blamelessness |
| **Closing** | "Is there anything in my background that gives you pause for this role?" | A chance to address a concern before the debrief |

Pick three or four; don't recite a list. Ask follow-up questions on their answers: a conversation is the signal, not the number of questions.

### 3.2 What to listen for

| Answer | Read it as |
|---|---|
| A specific, multi-year problem with business stakes | An L7 role |
| "We need someone to help the teams execute faster" | Possibly an L5/L6 role with an L7 title |
| A concrete example of a senior IC changing a decision | Real influence |
| "Architecture decisions are made by the architecture team" | Limited IC decision rights |
| A failure described with what changed | A learning culture |
| No failures, or failures blamed on individuals | A warning sign |
| A clear account of recent Staff+ promotions | A path exists |
| "We haven't promoted anyone to that level yet" | Ask why |

### 3.3 Questions that signal L5 when asked of a VP

These aren't wrong questions; they're questions for a different person in the loop:
- "What's the tech stack?" (ask an engineer)
- "What's the on-call rotation like?" (ask the team)
- "What would my first project be?" (implies you expect to be assigned work)
- "How is performance reviewed?" (ask the recruiter or hiring manager)

## 4. Hiring Committee Scoring Rubric

| Signal | Strong Hire at L7 | Down-level to L5 |
|---|---|---|
| **Pitch: identity** | Archetype, domain and scope in one sentence | A job title and years of experience |
| **Pitch: arc** | Scope grows at each step, with reasons | A chronological CV walk |
| **Pitch: proof** | One flagship headline with a business number | A list of technologies |
| **Level answer** | Evidence of operating at the level now | Aspiration only |
| **First 90 days** | Listen, choose, deliver one proof point; respects what came before | "I'd rewrite the architecture" |
| **Reverse questions** | About strategy, decision rights, the role's success and org health; follows up on answers | About stack, benefits and first tasks |
| **Closing** | Invites concerns and addresses them | No closing |

## 5. Story Blueprints

### 5.1 "Tell me about yourself"

#### How an L5 tells it

> "Sure. I have about twelve years of experience. I started at a consulting company working on Java applications, then moved to a retailer where I worked on the order management system, and then to my current company where I'm a senior engineer on the data platform team. I've worked with Kafka, Spark, Kubernetes and AWS. I'm looking for a new challenge and I'm interested in this role because it's a great company."

**What the committee extracts:** a CV walk, technologies instead of scope, and a generic reason. The interviewer learns nothing that frames the next hour. **L5.**

#### How an L7 tells it

> "I'm an engineer who works across teams on data platforms, mostly as an architect: finding the cross-team problem nobody owns, getting it funded, and getting a platform built that teams choose to adopt.
>
> I started in consulting, which taught me to influence clients I had no authority over. At a retailer, I moved from owning the order system to leading the migration of order capture for 1,200 stores off on-premises infrastructure, which was my first multi-team program. For the last three years I've been at a marketplace company, where my scope grew from one data team to technical direction for the four teams that make up our data platform.
>
> The work I'm proudest of: a year before our marketplace launch, I found that event volume would grow about tenfold and break our inventory-accuracy commitments to sellers. I proposed and led a cell-based event platform; eight teams run on it now, and oversell incidents went to near zero through the launch.
>
> I'm looking for a larger version of that problem: an org where data platforms are central to the business and nobody yet owns the whole picture. From what I've read about this org's plans for real-time personalization, that sounds like the problem here."

**What separates them:** identity with archetype and scope; an arc where scope grows and each step has a reason; one flagship headline with a business result; a close that connects to the interviewer's org.

### 5.2 "What would you do in your first 90 days?"

#### How an L5 tells it

> "I'd get to know the codebase and the team, and then start contributing as soon as possible. I'd look for areas where the architecture could be improved, and propose a modernization plan. I'd probably start with the services that have the most tech debt."

**What the committee extracts:** a code-first, team-level plan; architecture change proposed before listening. **L5, with a mild Respect-What-Came-Before concern.**

#### How an L7 tells it

> "For the first month, I'd listen. I'd meet every TL and the key partners in product, SRE and finance, and read the last year of incidents, the cost data and the main architecture decisions, including why they were made. At the end of the month I'd write a short map of what I think the top technical risks and opportunities are, and check it with you, because I'll have got some of it wrong.
>
> In the second month, I'd choose one problem that's important, has no clear owner, and could show a result within a quarter, and write a proposal with a first proof point and what it would displace.
>
> By day 90, I'd want that first proof point delivered, working through the teams who'd own it, and the longer plan agreed. The proof point matters more than its size: it's how the teams decide whether it's worth working with me on the bigger things."

**What separates them:** listening first, including to why past decisions were made; a written map checked with the manager; one chosen problem with a proof point delivered through teams; awareness of how credibility is earned.

### 5.3 Closing the interview

#### How an L5 tells it

> "I think I've covered everything. Thanks for your time, I really enjoyed the conversation."

#### How an L7 tells it

> "One question before we finish: is there anything in my background, or in what I've told you today, that gives you pause for this role? I'd rather address it now than have it come up in the debrief without me."
>
> (Interviewer: "Most of your scale has been smaller than ours.")
>
> "That's fair: our peak was about 40,000 requests a second. The hard part of my work was correctness across stores with unreliable connectivity, and the principles I used there, ordered idempotent events, explicit conflict rules agreed with the business, cells for blast radius, are the ones I'd expect to apply here. I'd also want to learn from the people here who've run multi-region systems at your scale. Is that the main gap you'd want covered?"

**What separates them:** the L7 invites the concern, addresses it with the six-step response (06.3 §3), and checks whether it's resolved, turning a silent debrief risk into a conversation.

## 6. Bar-Raiser Follow-Up Chains

| # | Probe | Tests | Strong response |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Why are you leaving?" | Positivity and honesty | Forward-looking: the problem you want, which your current role doesn't have |
| 2 | "Why this level and not one below?" | Calibration | Evidence of operating at the level now |
| 3 | "What would your current manager say is your biggest weakness?" | Self-awareness | A real one, with what you're doing about it |
| 4 | "Which of your career moves was a mistake?" | Reflection | An honest one, with what it taught |
| 5 | "What kind of problem do you not want to work on?" | Self-knowledge | A specific answer with a reason |
| 6 | "What would make you leave this role in two years?" | Motivation | An honest condition, such as "if the org-level problem turned out not to exist" |
| 7 | "What did you think of my answer to your question?" (after a reverse question) | Candor with a senior leader | A thoughtful, honest reaction |

---

## 7. Checklist

- [ ] A two-minute pitch with identity, arc, proof and "now", timed.
- [ ] A 30-second version.
- [ ] Variants for the hiring manager, the behavioral interviewer and the VP.
- [ ] Honest, positive answers to "why leave?" and "why this level?".
- [ ] A listen–choose–deliver answer to the first-90-days question.
- [ ] Three or four reverse questions for each senior interviewer, chosen for them, plus follow-ups.
- [ ] A closing question that invites concerns.
